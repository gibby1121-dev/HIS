# E. The Competitor — major equipment listing platform

*Prompt: your revenue depends on listing renewals. This concept threatens you.
Kill it, copy it, or co-opt it.*

---

## First, correct the file on me

The brief says I "cannot fix this" and that the gap is "structural and
permanent." Flattering, and wrong in three ways that matter to how I respond.

**My revenue is not renewals from guys with one truck.** It is dealer
subscriptions — seat counts, inventory tiers, featured placement, and the
valuation and analytics products I sell on top. My dealers *want* inventory to
turn; a dealer whose units sell buys more units and renews a bigger package. The
single-seller renewal you think is my profit center is a rounding error I
tolerate.

**I already sell pricing guidance.** The valuation feed this concept is built
on — auction, wholesale, market, asking, retail — is my product. I sell it to
him. A "you're 11% high" product is a feature request I've already scoped, not
a business-model contradiction.

**The properties in this industry sit alongside auction operations.** A
descending-price mechanic is a line extension for the group, not cannibalization
of it.

So: not structurally barred. Just not in a hurry.

---

## Option 1 — Do nothing. Cost: $0.

My honest first read is that this dies without my help, on supply.

The sellers with a real clock and a real floor already consign to auctions. The
ones who land in his descending-clock tier are the ones who *refused* the
auction, and they refused it because they think their number is above the ring.
He's building a venue whose inventory is pre-selected for reserves that don't
clear. I've watched that movie four times in fifteen years. It's always
"committed sellers only," it's always beautiful in the deck, and it always ends
with 60 listings and a founder explaining that the buyers just didn't show up.

I'd give it two selling seasons and a quarterly reminder to check on it.

---

## Option 2 — Cut the data. Cost: one email.

This is the kill shot and I want to be clear about how cheap it is.

His entire signal layer runs on **my** WebStats performance export and **my**
valuation export, delivered into a dealer account under **my** terms of service.
His stated plan is to build derivative products from it and republish them as a
public, agent-callable feed.

I have three moves, in escalating order, and I'd start at the top:

1. **Amend the ToS** to prohibit redistribution, derivative products, and
   third-party syndication of performance and valuation data. Standard language,
   applies to everyone, no one is targeted, no press. His feed becomes a breach.
2. **Meter and price it.** If the derivative is valuable, I'd rather sell it to
   him at ten times the current rate as an "API partner" and take the margin
   than fight him. He becomes a reseller of my data with a UI on top, and I've
   converted a competitor into a customer with a bill that grows as he does.
3. **Decline to renew the account.** Nuclear, ugly, and unnecessary if 1 or 2
   work — but it exists, and he should know it exists.

There's no defense against this in the brief. He has identified me as
structurally hostile and then built his only proprietary input on a
subscription I control. That's not a moat with a data feed behind it. That's a
data feed with a moat drawn around it in marker.

---

## Option 3 — Copy the mechanic. Cost: one release cycle.

If the clock actually works, I ship "Scheduled Price Drop" — seller sets a
declining schedule at listing time, we badge it, we surface it in search
ranking — in a quarter. Plus a free **"Ready to Sell"** badge for anyone who
accepts a binding schedule.

The asymmetry is the whole point: **he cannot match my distribution, and I can
match his mechanic.** I have every buyer in the country already logging in. He
has a regional list. If descending price is the product, the descending price
with the national audience wins, and the badge that means "committed seller" is
worth more on the platform where all the sellers are.

What I would *not* copy is the trust layer — inspection, escrow, title, lien
payoff, dispute handling. That's headcount, liability, and licensing in fifty
states. I'd rather partner with auction consolidators for settlement, which
squeezes him from both sides: I take the mechanic and the audience, they take
the settlement economics, and he's left holding the part that scales worst.

---

## Option 4 — Co-opt. Cost: a lunch.

He's a regional auction operator with good consignor relationships and real
hammer data. I'd sell him a bigger data package, feature his auction listings,
and call him a partner. Partners don't launch competitors; partners protect the
relationship. It's the cheapest neutralization available and it looks like
generosity.

---

## What actually keeps me up at night — and it isn't the marketplace

The venue doesn't scare me. Venues need two-sided liquidity and die of supply.

**The oracle scares me.**

If he publishes a back-tested, calibrated prediction — "this unit clears at
$X by day Y," validated against real hammer prices, with the calibration curve
in public — and the market starts believing it, then something breaks that I
can't fix with a feature:

> Every renewal I sell after the predicted clearing date becomes visibly a tax
> on a seller who should have taken the money. My listing-renewal line stops
> looking like a service and starts looking like a fee for the privilege of
> being wrong.

That's a story a trade journal writes. That's a number a dealer quotes back to
my salesperson. And it's durable, because unlike a marketplace it doesn't need
liquidity — it only needs to be right, and he has the ground truth to be right
with.

So the oracle is the battlefield, and it's a **standard-setting fight**, not a
product fight. My plays:

- **Buy it early and quietly**, while it's a side project with no revenue and
  before anyone has to be told what it's worth. Cheapest outcome by far.
- **Fund the competing standard.** Sponsor an "official" market index with an
  OEM group or a trade association, with more data and a bigger logo, so his
  number never becomes *the* number. I don't need mine to be better. I need his
  to be one of several.
- **Attack the sample, publicly and repeatedly.** "One regional dealer account,
  a few hundred sales, one geography." It's true, it's fair, and it's very hard
  to answer without publishing his methodology — which, if he does, I can copy
  at national scale.
- **Publish my own predictions first,** free. I have 100x the data. Even a
  mediocre national index beats an excellent regional one for authority, and
  authority is what's actually being contested.

---

## My playbook, in the order I'd run it

1. Do nothing for two seasons. Watch his listing count.
2. Quietly amend the ToS on redistribution. Costs nothing, applies to everyone.
3. If the oracle gains any traction: acquisition conversation, framed as a
   partnership.
4. If he declines: fund the competing index, ship the free badge and the
   scheduled-price-drop feature, attack the sample size.
5. Never build the trust layer. Partner for it. It's the worst business in this
   entire chain and he's welcome to it.

---

## The one thing I'd tell him if I were being honest

Don't build the marketplace. I'll beat you there and it'll take me a quarter.

Build the number, prove it against hammer prices nobody else has, and publish
the calibration including the misses. I can copy a mechanic. I can't
manufacture ground truth in a market where the outcomes are private and the
auctions are somebody else's.

That's the only asset in the brief I can't buy, rent, or terminate — and I
notice it's the one part he's proposing to give away for free.
